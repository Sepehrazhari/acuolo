The same thing, in four places

A client exists in the CRM, the project tool, the invoicing system and a spreadsheet. An employee exists in the HR tool, the payroll export and someone's tracker. They disagree, and no one can say which is right without checking all of them.

The stack tax

Every tool bills per seat, per month, forever, and each one charges you more precisely as you hire. Five subscriptions at a modest per-head price quietly becomes one of your larger line items, and none of it ever becomes an asset you own.

The person-shaped dependency

Someone clever wires it together with Airtable, Notion and automations. It works, until they leave and nobody else understands the permissions, the triggers, or why that column exists.

What makes this different

Three things, in the order they usually matter to the people making the decision.

01

It fits the processes you already run

We start from how your company actually works — your stages, your approvals, your definitions — and build to that. If your promotion process has a calibration step no product supports, or your delivery process has an approval that exists only in someone's head, it gets built. You stop maintaining the gap between the tools and reality, which is where most operational admin time actually goes.

02

You own it instead of renting it

One build, no per-seat licence. Hiring ten more people does not increase what the system costs you. And because no subscription holds it hostage, it keeps working whether or not you keep paying anyone — including us.

Do the sum on your own numbers

Add up every per-seat tool you pay for each month — the HR system, the project tool, the CRM, the rest. Multiply the total by your headcount. Multiply by thirty-six. That is your three-year cost for software you will never own, and it rises with every hire. Compare that with a one-time build. We would rather you ran that arithmetic yourself than took our word for it.

03

Your data stays in your own cloud

The system is deployed into your own cloud account. Employee records, client data, salary history and commercial information stay inside infrastructure you control, rather than spread across several vendors. For EU companies that removes most of the awkward part of a GDPR conversation: you decide where it is hosted and who can reach it, and there is no set of third-party processors to justify.

When this is the wrong choice

Headcount is not the test — small companies with unusual processes often benefit sooner than large ones with conventional ones. These are the real disqualifiers.

  • You need payroll, tax or statutory accounting. These are regulated, jurisdiction-specific and change constantly. Use specialist providers and integrate with them. Be wary of anyone offering to build you one.
  • Off-the-shelf genuinely fits. If your processes are conventional and existing products cover them without workarounds, buy the products. Paying for custom to arrive at the same place is a waste.
  • You need something running next week. Signing up for a SaaS tool takes an afternoon. Building takes weeks. If the deadline is immediate, buy something now and revisit this later.
  • Your processes are still changing weekly. A custom system encodes decisions. If you have not settled how things work yet, wait until they stop moving — otherwise you are paying to set today's guess in stone.
  • Nobody internally will own it. Any system needs someone whose job includes keeping it current. If there is no such person, external products with support lines are the safer choice.

Questions people ask

What is a company operating system?

A single internal system that runs how your organisation actually works, rather than a collection of separate products each covering one slice. It typically holds people records, clients and projects, internal processes and reporting in one place, so the same person, client or project exists once instead of in four disconnected tools. Built custom, it encodes the processes you already use rather than imposing a vendor's model.

How is this different from buying Personio, Asana and a CRM?

Those are good products, but each is designed around its own model of how you should work, each bills per seat every month, and none of them knows about the others. The cost is the subscriptions plus the seams between them: the same client or employee maintained in several places, and the manual work of keeping them agreeing. Studio replaces the seams with one system, built once, that you own.

Why not just build it in Airtable or Notion?

Airtable and Notion are good at structured lists and documents, and many companies get a long way with them. They tend to break down on process logic: permissions that differ by role, steps that must happen in a set order, calculations that have to be right, and anything that needs to run without someone remembering to trigger it. They also charge per seat, and the setup usually lives in one person's head until that person leaves.

How much does it cost?

It is quoted per project after a discovery call, because cost depends entirely on scope. The useful comparison is your own current spend: add up every per-seat tool you pay for, multiply the total by headcount, multiply by thirty-six months. That is your three-year cost for software you will never own.

Who owns the system and the data?

You do, including the source. The system is deployed into your own cloud account, so company and employee data stays inside infrastructure you control rather than a vendor's. For companies in the EU this simplifies GDPR questions considerably, because you determine where data is hosted and who can reach it.

Can it just cover HR and people management?

Yes, and that is a common starting point. People operations — records, hiring, reviews, progression, leave — is usually where off-the-shelf tools fit worst, so it is a sensible first module. The system can then extend to clients, projects and internal process without another migration, because it was built to be extended.

Is this only worth it for large companies?

No — headcount is the wrong test. What matters is whether your processes are unusual enough that off-the-shelf tools force workarounds, and how many per-seat subscriptions you already pay for tools that each only partly fit. Small companies with distinctive processes often benefit sooner than large ones with conventional ones.

Does it handle payroll or accounting?

No, and you should be wary of anyone who says otherwise. Payroll, tax and statutory accounting are regulated, jurisdiction-specific and change constantly, so they belong with specialist providers. A custom system integrates with them rather than replacing them.

What happens when we need changes later?

Because you own the system and its source, changes can be made by us, by another developer, or by your own team. Ongoing support and new features are available as an optional subscription, but nothing stops working if you decline it.

Tell us what does not fit.

The intake form takes about two minutes and asks what you use today, what breaks, and what you need it to handle. We read it and reply personally. If it is not a fit, we will tell you that too.
